Under the glass · what the pictures mean

The white body makes one lap per integer. A prime p gets a panel spanning 1/p of its circle, printed continuously between beats p and p + 1. It turns once every p beats. Its leading edge returns to the red meeting line at multiples of p. True spacing uses radius p relative to the growing frontier: all completed panels have equal arc length at a given time. The view zooms out as the count grows.

The product chapter uses unique factorization: choosing a power from each prime’s geometric series produces each positive integer exactly once. The sum and Euler product converge absolutely for Re(s) > 1. The decorative sieve-density spiral in the drawer is a different product, ∏(1 − 1/p), measured over a complete residue cycle.

In the complex view, up is real and right is imaginary. The arrows have lengths n−σ and angles −τ log n; this second clock belongs to the weights. It does not change the beat or the mechanical panels. Mint is the finite sum, coral adds x1−s/(s−1), and gold is a numerical estimate of ζ(s). At integer frontiers the coral-corrected endpoint tends to ζ(s) for σ > 0, except s = 1. The ordinary sum does not converge in the critical strip. The gold point uses Euler–Maclaurin with six correction terms. This picture motivates analytic continuation; it is not a proof of it.

The final chapter counts prime powers with weight log p, giving Chebyshev’s ψ(x). Its displayed zero vectors approximate the explicit formula, with midpoint values at jumps and ringing from truncation. Thirty known zero heights are supplied, not discovered by the clock. This is a bridge toward harmonic prime counting; ψ is not the unweighted count π or Riemann’s prime-power count J.

A small detour: why −2, −4, −6 are zeros

The continuation obeys a reflection formula. On the negative real axis, one factor is a turning hand’s vertical projection: sin(πs/2). At each negative even integer that projection is zero; the other factors are finite and nonzero.

ζ(s) = 2s πs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1−s) ζ(1−s)

This symmetry comes from the functional equation; the finite orbit construction does not derive it. Reflection formula · NIST
