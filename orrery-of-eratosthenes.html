Under the glass · what the pictures mean

The second hand makes one lap per integer. A prime p gets a panel spanning 1/p of its circle, printed continuously between beats p and p + 1. It turns once every p beats. Its leading edge returns to the red meeting line at multiples of p. True spacing uses radius p relative to the growing frontier: all completed panels have equal arc length at a given time. The view zooms out as the count grows. Each q = p^k has a full panel of width 2π/q in the same glass as p. At beat n, one pane is crossed for each prime-power divisor; the number of p-panes is the exponent of p. The scalar weight is the product of p^−σ across those panes. Displayed RGB colours encode the factor mixture, not calibrated physical luminance.

The product chapter follows Euler’s sieve: each factor (1 − p⁻ˢ) removes the surviving multiples of p from the series. The tape’s right side is the product of those factors times ζ(s). The panel for p still occupies its original 1/p slot; no repeated-factor construction is asserted by this chapter. The ordinary sum and Euler product converge absolutely for Re(s) > 1. In the critical strip the tape shows finite terms only, not a convergent infinite equality.

In clock orientation, up is real and right is imaginary. Reflection swaps these axes: right becomes real and up becomes imaginary; the second hand then moves counterclockwise. Labels remain upright. Reflection changes only the view, not the count or arithmetic. The arrows have lengths n−σ and angles −τ log n; this second clock belongs to the weights. It does not change the beat or the mechanical panels. Mint is the finite sum, coral adds x1−s/(s−1), and gold is a numerical estimate of ζ(s). At integer frontiers the coral-corrected endpoint tends to ζ(s) for σ > 0, except s = 1. The ordinary sum does not converge in the critical strip. The gold point uses Euler–Maclaurin with six correction terms. Geometry lets the sum move continuously onto its own labelled ruler. The clock radius is a position assigned to an integer; the sum link is the complex value n⁻ˢ. Shared beat and factor colours connect them, not an equality of radii. The optional outward ruler is log(t/q) / log(t/2), easing in over beats 2–3 where that range first opens. It changes displayed arc lengths, not the panel periods. For nonzero σ the same ratio comes from log weights, with σ cancelling; σ = 0 uses that limiting ruler. This picture motivates analytic continuation; it is not a proof of it.

The final chapter counts prime powers with weight log p, giving Chebyshev’s ψ(x). Its displayed zero vectors approximate the explicit formula, with midpoint values at jumps and ringing from truncation. Thirty known zero heights are supplied, not discovered by the clock. This is a bridge toward harmonic prime counting; ψ is not the unweighted count π or Riemann’s prime-power count J.

A small detour: why −2, −4, −6 are zeros

The continuation obeys a reflection formula. On the negative real axis, one factor is a turning hand’s vertical projection: sin(πs/2). At each negative even integer that projection is zero; the other factors are finite and nonzero.

ζ(s) = 2s πs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1−s) ζ(1−s)

This symmetry comes from the functional equation; the finite orbit construction does not derive it. Reflection formula · NIST
